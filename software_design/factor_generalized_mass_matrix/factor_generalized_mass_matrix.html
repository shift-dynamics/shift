Factoring the Constrained Generalized Mass Matrix¶

In this note we review procedures for recursively sparse factoring a generalized mass matrix with appended preconditioned constraint equations and solving for the unknowns. Preconditioning the constraint equations takes place in three steps described below.

But first, what do we mean by preconditioning constraint equations? The Harwell Subroutine Library (HSL) http://www.hsl.rl.ac.uk/ provides specialized functions to sparse factor and solve linear systems of equations of many forms. The HSL MA67 package http://www.hsl.rl.ac.uk/catalogue/ma67.html sparse factors a symmetric matrix with zeros on the diagonal into the form , where is block diagonal with blocks of size 2 and 1, and where one or both entries on the diagonal of the 2 by 2 matrices may be 0. The logic to put the equations into this form represents a considerable amount of extra overhead, and the constraint equations associated with these pivot rows must be scaled in association with inverting the pivotal matrices.

It isn’t clear from the documentation in the above link whether MA67 will handle a singular matrix, but if it doesn’t, then HSL has another similar version that will. When the constraint equations do not have full row rank, the MA67 or alternate package must expend considerably more effort and expense splitting the equations into primary and secondary sets. Of course, the bulk factorization and solver parts of the package are quite efficient, but the whole process can still be quite complex.

Here we take a different approach by preconditioning the constraint equations before appending them to the generalized equations of motion. In this case, each 2 by 2 pivot submatrix will contain two 1s off diagonal and one or two 0s on diagonal. Because the determinant of these 2 by 2 pivot matrices is always , no additional scaling or modification of the constraint equations is ever required. Also, since the preconditioned constraint equations have full row rank, the coefficient matrix to be factored will have full rank for all well-posed problems. Preconditioning the constraint equations up front greatly simplifies the factorization and solver algorithms and reduces the logical overhead to nearly zero. In essence, constraint preconditioning has split the constraint factorization away from the constrained equations of motion to simplify these algorithms. Since external constraint preconditioning is required for other purposes as well, no efficiency is sacrificed in performing it separately.

The first preconditioning step divides the model into kinematic substructures, where the constrained joint variables within a substructure are not in any other substructure. Thus each substructure will have its own set of constraint equations, joint variables, and Jacobian matrix. When the generalized equations of motion and preconditioned substructures are properly arranged, the block of equations and variables associated with the current substructure may be processed as if all remaining substructures didn’t exist. With this observation, in the uncoupling and solution process, we may temporarily ignore the kinematic constraint equations for all but the current substructure being processed. We will illustrate this shortly.

Thus the equations for just one kinematic substructure are required for developing and illustrating the optimal uncoupling and solution methods. In addition, when uncoupling the equations and solving for one dependent variable and its associated Lagrange multiplier within the remaining substructure, these processes do not depend on or modify any of the remaining preconditioned constraint equations. Thus we may also temporarily ignore the remaining portion of the substructure’s preconditioned constraint equations, which further simplifies developing and describing the factorization and solution processes.

The GECP process has effectively accomplished Generalized Coordinate Partitioning (GCP) within each kinematic substructure by permuting and splitting its joint variables into dependent and independent sets. Rearranging the variables associated with each substructure into dependent and independent submatrices and likewise within the composite array of variables requires permuting the rows and columns of the generalized mass matrix as well. This is addressed in the third preconditioning step.

The third preconditioning step rearranges the variables, constraint equations, and generalized equations of motion, placing all independent variables before the dependent variables within each kinematic substructure. We first note that optimal equation and variable ordering within a set of unconstrained generalized equations of motion with positive definite mass matrix yields zero fills in the factorization process and minimizes the operations count. However, the constrained generalized mass matrix is allowed to be singular for well-posed problems. Because the constrained generalized mass matrix is generally singular, robust and stable algorithms require a modified solution order that produces fills and reduces efficiency. The challenge is to find a good tradeoff between robustness, stability, and efficiency, which is the primary goal of this discussion.

Before GECP is applied for the first time to the Jacobian matrices to precondition the constraint equations and obtain GCP, the equations and variables in the generalized equations of motion are optimally ordered to minimize fills. However, as indicated in the previous paragraph, the constrained equations of motion are rarely uncoupled in this order because the generalized mass matrix may be singular, and attempting to invert too small pivots or zero pivots must be avoided. Many researchers, striving to uncouple the constrained equations of motion in optimal unconstrained order, may employ tricks to obtain positive definite generalized mass matrices. Factoring such modified matrices in this order may result in poorly conditioned solutions and errors. Furthermore, these errors may be difficult or impossible to detect, and they may destroy robustness, stability, and efficiency.

To avoid such problems and eliminate the possibility of encountering zero pivots for well-posed models, all dependent variables within the kinematic substructure being processed must be eliminated before starting on the first independent variable. To minimize the number of fills in the uncoupling process, all dependent variables within each substructure must remain in their original unconstrained optimal order, and only the independent variables may be moved ahead of the corresponding dependent variables. The final order of independent variables within each substructure doesn’t matter, because the number of fills will be the same for any order. Keeping the independent variables in natural order is one option, which is used here. This simply means that the independent variables within a substructure are shifted forward but not interchanged in the process. To maintain symmetry within the generalized mass matrix, the equations of motion must be permuted into the same order as the rearranged variables.

When the constrained generalized equations of motion are initially set up, their equations and variables are arranged into the same order as the bodies and joints in the spanning trees. This means that the equations and variables for bodies and joints with smaller indices come before the others. The optimal uncoupling and solution order for this arrangement is then backward elimination and forward substitution, with indexing order following the spanning trees. Thus the matrix is factored contiguously from bottom right to top left, which is opposite the method normally described in the literature.

In the composite set of constrained equations of motion, if one or more independent variables and unconstrained equations of motion come after the last kinematic substructure, these variables may be eliminated and solved for, independent of all constraint equations. This means that all preconditioned constraint equations may be ignored during the recursive factoring and solve steps for these and all subsequent independent variables that come at later stages. However, we first must demonstrate how to factor the sparse, generally singular generalized mass matrix , whose lower triangular part on and below the diagonal is stored in a linear array. Since fills will be generated in the factorization process, we cannot use the parent-child list to determine the location of nonzero entries or to figure out where to assign updated terms. Instead we will use a linear Boolean array, which will have the same size as the linear array holding the generalized mass matrix.

The AOP will use a permuted version of the parent-child list, expanded to primitive joint variable resolution, to initialize the Boolean array at the same time it generates the destination pointers for assigning all nonzero entries to the linear array containing the generalized mass matrix. It will assign a 1 or true to each location in the Boolean array where an entry would be assigned to the array containing the mass matrix . Then, as the AOP goes through the symbolic steps of factoring , it will symbolically process the Boolean array, checking each accessed entry for 1 or true to determine if an operation should be performed. And each time an assignment would be made to the array holding , the AOP will assign a 1 or true to that corresponding location in the Boolean array.

A second Boolean array initially represents the sparsity pattern of each kinematic substructure’s Jacobian matrix, and eventually part of it will represent the sparsity pattern of the matrix. The AOP will use this Boolean array to locate nonzero entries in , and it will set up operations using only those entries in corresponding to the location of 1 or true entries found in its Boolean array representation. As discussed earlier, the rows and columns of the matrices are not in the order required for appending directly to the generalized equations of motion, but they are in the order determined by GECP and generated by the sparse factor and solve algorithm. To access the matrix entries, which are stored in the linear array holding all Jacobian matrices, the AOP will need to generate some offset information and another permutation array.

Before describing the sparse evaluate and factor algorithm to be applied to the constrained generalized equations of motion, it will be helpful to review the three levels of model setup and processing, denoted here as L1 through L3.

In level L1, kinematic substructures are identified and bodies and joints are arranged by substructure to block diagonalize the Jacobian matrix. A parent-child list is generated to represent the relationship between all bodies and joints. In L1 is where AOPs identify initial algorithms that will evaluate the generalized equations of motion and the constraint equations, which have been partitioned into kinematic substructures.

GECP is applied to the Jacobian matrices evaluated in L1 to identify generalized coordinate partitioning and precondition the kinematic substructure constraint equations. The GECP process effectively reorders the constraint equations and joint variables from L1 to define L2. This requires new equation and variable permutation arrays. The primary Jacobian matrices are stored and uncoupled in sparse form in L2 order, and the primary constraint equations are stored in L2 order. These operations require a permutation array from L1 to L2, which is defined by the GECP algorithm.

In L3, the equations and variables in the generalized equations of motion from L1 are symmetrically rearranged to move independent variables ahead of dependent variables within each kinematic substructure. This requires a permutation array from L1 to L3. In addition, the rows and columns of each substructure’s matrix must be permuted from L2 to align with the rearranged dependent and independent variables in L3. This requires another permutation array from L2 to L3. Thus L3 requires two permutation arrays, one from L1 and another from L2.

In L2 is where most of the RTA action on kinematic substructures takes place. An RTA, running exclusively in L2, evaluates the primary part of each kinematic substructure’s Jacobian matrix and constraint equations, sparse factors the matrices, and evaluates the matrices and other quantities. Prior to this, an AOP will have used various permutation arrays and parent-child lists to set up arrays of source and destination pointers that the RTA will use in L2 to access and store data in appropriate levels as it carries out these operations. Each computed matrix is evaluated and stored in L2, because the algorithms running in this level should not have to know in advance about the permuted orders in L3. Since the elements of were not permuted directly to L3 as they were evaluated in L2, the AOP will generate source pointers that will allow the RTA, running in L3, to directly access the entries generated and stored in L2. There will be no overhead to the RTA in doing this.

A similar situation holds for evaluating and processing the constrained generalized equations of motion. Back in L1, an AOP will have used previous permutation arrays and the L1 parent-child list to develop arrays of source and destination pointers for evaluating the generalized mass matrix and equations of motion. In L1 the AOP stops there. In L3 the AOP identifies and uses new permutation arrays to redirect the destination memory pointers in the permuted generalized mass matrix to new locations. In essence, the generalized mass matrix in L3 is evaluated in the same order defined in L1, but its evaluated quantities are stored directly in permuted order according to the new destination memory pointer arrays generated for L3. This method eliminates unnecessary movement of data in memory. The AOP, indexing in L3, will use the appropriate permutation array to access and modify these quantities directly in L1 as needed. As will be discussed later, accessing and changing quantities between levels will be a common way of avoiding copying results between levels. In all previous and subsequent discussions, when talking about processing data in one level that exist in other levels, we imply that appropriate permutations between the levels are being used.

With all matrices evaluated in L2, the permuted generalized mass matrix and equations of motion evaluated in L3, all numerical quantities are in place for the RTA to factor and solve the constrained generalized equations of motion. But it can’t do that until the AOP has generated the necessary source and destination memory pointer arrays. In essence, the AOP must step through the entire process just once, using all the logic and permutation arrays and Boolean matrices it needs to factor and solve these generalized equations of motion with appended preconditioned constraints. However, the AOP won’t numerically factor the matrix and solve these equations, but it will go through the act by checking entries in the Boolean matrices and modifying them as needed. For each and every numerical operation that would be performed in the sparse factor and solve process, the AOP will identify the source and destination memory pointers needed to accomplish this and add them to the appropriate source and destination memory pointer arrays. When finished, the AOP will have gone through the entire process of sparse factoring and solving the constrained generalized equations of motion with preconditioned constraints, and without having computed a single number. But the source and destination memory pointer arrays and other necessary information will be ready for the RTA to compute many numbers.

With this background we are about ready to describe how the AOP will accomplish these tasks. The AOP-generated procedures to evaluate the generalized mass matrix and equations of motion in L1 and the AOP-generated procedures to evaluate and process the constraint equations and matrices in L2 were detailed elsewhere and won’t be covered here. Thus we won’t describe how each entry in the generalized mass matrix is evaluated in L1, but we will describe how the AOP will use the parent-child list to determine where each entry would go in L1, because it has to use this information to figure out where the entries will be redirected in L3.

This factorization algorithm will function differently than previous ones, because fills will create additional entries on and below the diagonal in , in places where new nonzero pivots and new entries in the matrix factors are stored. The parent-child list cannot detect these fills, and it wouldn’t apply to this permuted matrix anyway. Instead the AOP must cycle through the updated Boolean array MB[] to determine where each nonzero entry in , stored in Ml[], is located. Likewise it must cycle through JB[] to determine where each nonzero entry in , stored in J[], is located. As the symbolic factorization progresses, the AOP will update entries in MB[] to record each assignment to Ml[] and potential new fills. At each step of the factorization process, the entries in MB[] will reflect the exact sparsity pattern of the modified matrix. When the factorization process is complete, MB[] will define exactly the sparsity pattern of the and matrices, and the AOP will then use the sparsity patterns in MB[] and JB[] to generate the source and destination pointer arrays for solving the constrained equations of motion.

The AOP must navigate through the MB[], JB[], Ml[], and J[] arrays to set up arrays of source and destination pointers that the RTA will use to sparse factor and solve the equations. The MB[] and Ml[] arrays are fairly easy to navigate, but the JB[] and J[] arrays will require some additional work. The constraint equations and Jacobian matrices were permuted and block partitioned into kinematic substructures in L1, where each substructure has its own set of constraint equations, its own block of variables, and its own Jacobian matrix.

The single array J[] is set up with fixed offsets to hold all of the kinematic substructure Jacobian submatrices. We use the correct fixed offset into J[] to access each element of the current kinematic substructure’s Jacobian matrix. For each kinematic substructure indexed in L2, the AOP initially evaluates its Jacobian matrix in L1 and applies GECP to factor it, and occasionally thereafter the AOP may evaluate the Jacobian matrix in L1 and apply GECP to factor it.

From the GECP’s row and column permutation results, the AOP sets up arrays of source and destination pointers, which the RTA will then use to sparse evaluate, factor, and solve only the primary equations in L2. The current variable can be one of three basic types: an independent variable with nonzero mass on the diagonal, a dependent variable with nonzero mass on the diagonal, or a dependent variable with zero mass on the diagonal. This RTA algorithm to uncouple and solve the constrained equations of motion using the previous matrix factors is somewhat longer and more complex than the earlier factorization algorithm, but that is due to more variations in assignment statements. Yet it is still much simpler and quite more efficient than an algorithm using complex logic and integer indexing arrays. In addition, some source and destination memory pointer arrays are used more than once for savings in memory space.
